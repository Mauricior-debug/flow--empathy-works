Why people don’t leave jobs — they leave leadership

By EmpathyWorks Leadership Consultancy

People don’t leave bad jobs. They leave bad bosses.

It’s a phrase we’ve heard many times. But it’s more than a soundbite. It’s a reality quietly playing out in boardrooms, charities, clinics, and classrooms across the UK.

The data backs it up: the number one reason people cite for leaving a role isn’t pay. It’s the quality of leadership.

At EmpathyWorks, we’ve seen this play out again and again. People thrive in difficult roles with the right leader. And they disengage in dream roles when the leadership is toxic, absent, or performative.

Leadership, then, becomes the lens through which people experience their entire job. And when that lens is fogged by fear, indifference, or disconnection, people don’t just lose motivation. They lose trust.

What “bad leadership” really looks like

It’s not always the bully or the shouter. In fact, the most dangerous leadership styles are often more subtle:

  • Leaders who are emotionally unavailable
  • Leaders who micromanage trust out of their teams
  • Leaders who are conflict-avoidant or non-committal
  • Leaders who disappear when things get hard
  • Leaders who prioritise image over integrity
  • Leaders who hoard information or decision-making
  • Leaders who appear approachable but are emotionally inconsistent

These behaviours may not trigger immediate resignations. But they leave emotional imprints. Slowly, they chip away at psychological safety, motivation, and belonging.

This isn’t about demonising leaders. It’s about calling out a pattern — one many of us were never taught to see. In fact, many toxic behaviours in leadership are normalised as “strong management” or “high standards.”

But when leadership operates from fear — fear of losing control, fear of failure, fear of feedback — that fear trickles down. It shows up in how people speak, how they share ideas, and whether they show up fully at all.

When leaders aren’t equipped to show up with compassion, clarity, and consistency, they create environments where good people quietly leave. Or worse, stay — disengaged and demoralised.

What makes people stay?

We often assume people leave because they’re chasing something better. But more often, they leave because they no longer feel connected to what they’re doing or who they’re doing it for.

In our work with organisations, three things come up consistently as the reasons people stay, even when things are hard:

They feel seen. Their leader notices, listens, and understands — not just what they do, but how they’re doing.

They feel safe. They can raise issues, admit mistakes, and ask for help without fear of humiliation.

They feel stretched. They’re challenged, developed, and trusted with responsibility and growth.

These are human needs, not nice-to-haves. When these things are present, people will give their best. Not because they have to. Because they want to.

Trust, recognition, and growth turn the workplace from a transactional space into a meaningful one. And meaning is what retains talent.

Signs you have a leadership problem, not a talent problem

Before assuming you have a recruitment issue or a generational work ethic problem, pause and ask:

  • Are people leaving certain teams more than others?
  • Are exit interviews hinting at relational issues?
  • Are team members hesitant to speak up in meetings?
  • Are good performers withdrawing or burning out?
  • Are your leaders self-aware, coachable, and emotionally intelligent?

If the answer to some of these is yes, you might not have a talent drain — you may have a leadership gap.

And unlike job descriptions, leadership behaviour is rarely written down. It’s modelled. Repeated. Protected. Or left unchallenged.

So what can organisations do?

If your organisation is experiencing churn, burnout, or low engagement, it may be time to stop looking at the role and start looking at the leadership.

Start here:

  • Audit leadership behaviour as actively as you audit performance
  • Offer coaching and support, not just accountability and oversight
  • Create feedback loops that are regular, brave, and safe
  • Measure psychological safety, not just productivity
  • Reward relational intelligence, not just results

Create leadership development that prioritises how we lead — not just what we achieve. Because people don’t follow job descriptions. They follow leaders.

And the way leaders behave shapes culture faster than any strategic plan.

Compassion is a leadership skill

Compassion in leadership is not about being nice. It’s about being present, responsive, and courageous.

Compassion means:

  • Making hard decisions with humanity
  • Listening to what’s not being said
  • Balancing clarity with care
  • Holding others accountable while staying connected

Research shows that compassionate leadership leads to higher retention, lower burnout, and stronger team performance. People want to work where they feel they matter — not just as resources, but as humans.

Final thought

Leadership is a privilege. Done well, it transforms lives.

And when you get leadership right, retention isn’t a problem — it becomes your reputation.
